# Pregnancy Weight Gain Calculator

A pregnancy weight gain calculator is a tool that maps your pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) to the recommended total weight-gain range published by the Institute of Medicine (IOM, now the National Academy of Medicine) in 2009. Because a healthy gain depends on where you started, the guidelines set different ranges for underweight, normal-weight, overweight, and obese BMI categories, with separate ranges for twin pregnancies. This calculator computes your pre-pregnancy BMI, shows the IOM range for your category, and estimates the expected gain at your current week. It runs entirely in your browser and is an educational estimate, not a substitute for prenatal care.

## Use cases

### See your target range early in pregnancy

Knowing the IOM range that matches your pre-pregnancy BMI helps set realistic expectations from the first trimester. A person who started at a normal BMI has a different healthy target (about 11.5–16 kg) than someone who started overweight (about 7–11.5 kg), and seeing the right band avoids comparing yourself to the wrong number.

### Check expected gain at your current week

Gain is not linear — most happens after the first trimester. Entering your current week gives an estimated expected gain so far and how much of the recommended range remains, so a mid-pregnancy figure can be read in context rather than against the full-term total.

### Understand twin-pregnancy differences

Twin pregnancies have higher recommended gains, and the calculator switches to the IOM twin ranges when you select twins. It is also honest about a gap in the evidence: IOM 2009 gives no recommended range for underweight twin pregnancies, and the tool says so rather than inventing a number.

### Bring a concrete figure to a prenatal visit

Having your BMI category and IOM range ready makes it easier to ask your provider whether your personal target should differ — for example, because of a health condition. Your provider’s guidance always overrides a general guideline.

### Set realistic postpartum expectations

Pregnancy weight is not lost overnight — roughly the baby, placenta, and fluid leave at birth, while the rest comes off gradually over the following months. Seeing where your total gain fell within the IOM range gives a calmer, evidence-based starting point for the postpartum period, without pressure to return to a pre-pregnancy number on any fixed schedule.

## How it works

1. **Choose singleton or twins** — This selects which IOM 2009 range table applies; twin ranges are higher.
2. **Enter pre-pregnancy weight** — Type your weight before pregnancy in kg or lbs — the ranges are keyed to your starting point, not your current weight.
3. **Enter your height** — Provide height in cm or feet/inches so the tool can compute your pre-pregnancy BMI.
4. **Enter your current week** — Give a week from 1 to 42 to estimate expected gain so far and the remaining range.
5. **Read the recommendation** — The tool shows your BMI, its category, the IOM total-gain range, expected gain at your week, remaining gain, and an approximate weekly rate.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**Which guidelines does this use?**</summary>

The Institute of Medicine (IOM/NAM) 2009 recommendations, which set total pregnancy weight-gain ranges by pre-pregnancy BMI category for singleton and twin pregnancies.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Why does it ask for pre-pregnancy weight, not current weight?**</summary>

The IOM ranges are based on your BMI before pregnancy, which determines the recommended total gain. Your current weight is used only indirectly, through the expected-gain estimate for your week.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What about underweight twin pregnancies?**</summary>

IOM 2009 states there was insufficient evidence to set a range for underweight twin pregnancies. This tool shows your BMI category and clearly says no recommendation exists, so you can discuss a target with your provider.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is weight gain supposed to be steady?**</summary>

No. Little gain is expected in the first trimester (often around 0.5–2 kg total), with most of the gain occurring later. The expected-gain figure reflects this non-linear pattern approximately.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is this a substitute for my doctor’s advice?**</summary>

No. It is an educational estimate. Your healthcare provider may recommend a different target based on your health, and their guidance takes precedence.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does it store my details?**</summary>

No. Calculations happen in your browser and nothing is uploaded or saved.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Can I use pounds and feet/inches?**</summary>

Yes. Toggle the weight and height units; the tool converts internally to compute BMI in metric.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How much of the weight is lost at delivery?**</summary>

Only part of it. The baby, placenta, and amniotic fluid together usually account for several kilograms lost around birth; the remaining gain — extra blood volume, fluid, and fat stores — is released gradually over weeks to months, which is entirely normal.

</details>

## Tips

- Use your weight from just before pregnancy for the most accurate BMI category.
- Read the mid-pregnancy figure as a checkpoint, not the full-term goal.
- Focus on the recommended range rather than a single target number.
- Bring the BMI category and range to your prenatal visit and confirm your personal target.
- Remember gain is normal and expected — the guideline is about a healthy range, not restriction.
