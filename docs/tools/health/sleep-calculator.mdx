# Sleep Calculator

A sleep cycle calculator suggests bedtime or wake-time targets that align with 90-minute sleep cycles — the rough average length of one complete cycle through light sleep, deep sleep, and REM — so you wake at the end of a cycle (lighter sleep stage) rather than during deep sleep where waking is groggy. The ZTools Sleep Cycle Calculator works in both directions ("if I want to wake at 7 AM, when should I sleep?" and "if I sleep at 11 PM, when should my alarm go off?"), accounts for ~14 minutes of average sleep latency, and recommends 4–6 cycles (6 to 9 hours) per night with adults typically optimal at 5 cycles.

## Use cases

### Avoiding the groggy alarm

Set the alarm at a 90-minute boundary so you wake during light sleep, feeling clearer.

### Planning short naps

20-minute power naps (no full cycle) avoid sleep inertia. 90-minute naps complete one full cycle for deeper recovery.

### Shift-worker schedule planning

Non-traditional sleep windows still benefit from cycle alignment.

### Recovering from short sleep

When short on time, 4 cycles (6 hours) is better than 5 hours of partial-cycle sleep — better stage completion.

## How it works

1. **Pick direction** — Bedtime → wake time (start at a known bedtime), or wake time → bedtime (start from desired wake).
2. **Enter the known time** — Hour and minute. The calculator factors in ~14 minutes of sleep latency (time to fall asleep).
3. **List cycle boundaries** — Four to six options spaced 90 minutes apart. Each represents one more / fewer cycle.
4. **Pick the one that fits your schedule** — Most adults benefit from 5 cycles (~7.5 hours). 4 cycles (~6 hours) for short nights; 6 cycles (~9 hours) for catch-up.
5. **Use it** — Set the alarm; ideally combine with a wind-down routine 30+ min before bedtime.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**Are cycles really exactly 90 minutes?**</summary>

They average 90 minutes but vary from 70 to 110 minutes per person and across the night. The first cycle is often shorter; later ones longer. The 90-minute model is a useful simplification, not a precise rule.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Why does waking at a cycle boundary feel better?**</summary>

You wake during light sleep (NREM stage 1 or 2) rather than deep sleep (NREM stage 3) or REM, where waking causes "sleep inertia" and grogginess.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is 8 hours mandatory?**</summary>

Not universally. Adult needs range from 7 to 9 hours; some individuals function fine on less or need more. Optimise for how you feel during the day.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does sleep tracker data improve this?**</summary>

Yes — wearables that track stages can suggest wake times based on your real cycles rather than the population average.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What about naps?**</summary>

For short naps, 20 minutes avoids deep sleep entirely (no inertia). For longer recovery, complete 90 minutes for one full cycle. Avoid 30–60 minute naps — that is the inertia zone.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Will alcohol or screens disrupt cycles?**</summary>

Yes — alcohol fragments REM, blue light delays melatonin onset, and both reduce cycle quality. Cycle-boundary timing helps less if the cycles themselves are degraded.

</details>

## Tips

- Aim for 5 cycles (7.5 hours) as a default; adjust to feel.
- Avoid 30–60 minute naps; either 20 min or 90 min for best results.
- Combine cycle-boundary alarms with consistent sleep / wake times for compounding benefits.
- Track for a week to learn your personal cycle length — wearables can help.
- Block screens 30 min before bed and avoid alcohol within 3 hours — both improve cycle quality.
