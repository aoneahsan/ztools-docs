# compound-interest-calculator

A compound interest calculator projects the future value of an investment that earns interest on both the original principal and previously accumulated interest, optionally including regular contributions, across years and at any compounding frequency. The ZTools Compound Interest Calculator takes principal, monthly contribution, annual rate, years, and compounding frequency (daily/monthly/quarterly/yearly), then returns the future value, total contributions, total interest earned, and a year-by-year balance breakdown — making the long-term power of compounding visible.

## Use cases

### Retirement projection at age 25, 35, 45

Compare starting at 25 vs 35 with the same monthly contribution. The 10-year head start often doubles the final balance — concrete motivation to start saving early.

### College savings for a newborn

Project an 18-year horizon at 7% with $200/month. See if the plan covers in-state tuition; adjust the contribution if not.

### High-yield savings vs CDs vs index funds

Compare 4% (HYSA), 5% (CD), and 8% (long-term index average) over 20 years. The compounding gap is dramatic and informs allocation decisions.

### Comparing simple vs compound interest

Toggle between simple and compound to see the difference. On a 30-year horizon, compound interest can produce 3–4× the simple-interest result at the same rate.

## How it works

1. **Enter the starting principal** — The amount invested today. $0 is fine if you're starting from scratch and only making contributions.
2. **Enter monthly or annual contribution** — Optional: regular additions to the principal. Toggle between monthly and annual depending on how you actually contribute.
3. **Set the annual rate and time horizon** — Annual percentage yield (APY). Time in years. Use realistic long-term returns: stocks ~7% real, bonds ~3% real, savings accounts ~3–5% nominal.
4. **Choose compounding frequency** — Daily, monthly, quarterly, or yearly. Most checking/savings accounts compound daily; CDs monthly; mutual funds typically quarterly or annually.
5. **Read the future value and breakdown** — Total balance, total contributions, and total interest earned. Year-by-year table shows how interest accelerates over time.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**How does compound interest work?**</summary>

Each compounding period, the interest earned is added to the principal. The next period earns interest on the larger balance — so the growth accelerates. Over decades, this exponential effect is dramatic, which is why it's called "the eighth wonder of the world".

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What's a realistic interest rate to assume?**</summary>

For long-term stock market: 7% real (after inflation), 10% nominal — that's the historical S&P 500 average. For high-yield savings: 4–5% currently (rate-sensitive). For CDs: 4–6%. For bonds: 3–5%. Avoid inputting 15%+ unless you know your risk.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Why does compounding frequency matter?**</summary>

More frequent compounding earns slightly more interest. The difference between annual and daily compounding at 7% over 30 years is about 1.5% of the final balance — meaningful but not huge.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How do contributions change the math?**</summary>

Each contribution starts compounding the moment it's added. A $500 contribution made today has 30 years to grow; one made in year 29 has only 1 year. Front-loaded contributions are dramatically more valuable than late ones.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does this account for inflation?**</summary>

No — this calculator shows nominal future value. To estimate real (inflation-adjusted) future value, use a rate of (your rate − expected inflation), e.g., 7% nominal − 3% inflation = 4% real.

</details>

## Tips

- Time is more valuable than amount — starting 10 years earlier often beats contributing twice as much for a shorter time.
- Use the calculator to see your "magic number": how much you need to contribute monthly to hit your retirement goal.
- Tax-advantaged accounts (401k, IRA, Roth) compound without tax drag — favor them for long-term goals.
- Always compare APY, not APR — APY accounts for compounding frequency.
