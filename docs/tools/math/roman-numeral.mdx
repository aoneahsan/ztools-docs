# Roman Numeral Converter

A Roman numeral converter translates Arabic numbers (1, 2, 3…) to Roman numerals (I, II, III…) and back, following the standard subtractive notation rules used in clocks, copyrights, monarch numbering, and Super Bowl labels. The ZTools Roman Numeral Converter supports the standard range 1–3999 (basic notation) plus the extended range up to 3,999,999 using vinculum (overline) notation for thousands, validates input strictly, and explains the substring breakdown so users learn the rules instead of just trusting the result.

## Use cases

### Tattoo, jewelry, and date inscriptions

Convert a birthdate (e.g., 1987) to Roman numerals (MCMLXXXVII) for a custom tattoo or engraving. Verifies the artist got it right before commit-to-skin.

### Reading older publications and copyright dates

Old films, books, and BBC programmes use Roman numerals for copyright (MCMLXXXIV = 1984). The reverse converter decodes them instantly.

### Outline numbering and document hierarchy

Legal briefs, books, and academic papers use Roman numerals for chapter or section numbering. Convert to renumber sections after edits.

### Math and history homework

Students learn that 999 is not IM but CMXCIX, and that subtractive notation only applies to specific pairs. The breakdown teaches the rules.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**What are the rules for Roman numerals?**</summary>

Basic letters: I=1, V=5, X=10, L=50, C=100, D=500, M=1000. Subtraction is allowed only for specific pairs: IV (4), IX (9), XL (40), XC (90), CD (400), CM (900). No letter repeats more than 3 times in a row (so 4 is IV, not IIII; 9 is IX, not VIIII).

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Why is 4 written as IV and not IIII?**</summary>

The standard form is IV (one before five). However, on clock faces 4 is often written as IIII for visual symmetry — both are recognized but IV is the formal standard.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How do you write large numbers like 5000 or 1,000,000?**</summary>

Beyond 3999, the vinculum notation places an overline above letters to multiply by 1000: V̄ = 5000, M̄ = 1,000,000. The converter renders this with overlines and explains.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is there a Roman numeral for zero?**</summary>

No — Roman numerals have no symbol for zero. The medieval mathematicians used the word "nulla" or the letter N when needed, but it was never part of the formal numeral system.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Why do films use Roman numerals for copyright dates?**</summary>

Tradition stretching back to early 20th-century cinema. Some say it was originally to make the film look older or harder to date for re-release; today it's pure convention.

</details>

## Tips

- The clean way to convert mentally: thousands first (MMM = 3000), then hundreds, tens, ones — same as Arabic place value.
- For tattoos, double-check the conversion before the appointment — Roman numeral mistakes are notoriously common in body art.
- Always use the strictest form (IV not IIII, IX not VIIII) for formal documents.
- Beyond 3999, prefer Arabic numerals — vinculum notation is rare and unfamiliar to most readers.
