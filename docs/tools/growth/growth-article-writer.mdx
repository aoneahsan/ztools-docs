## Use cases

### Pillar post from a rough brief

A SaaS content marketer needs an 1,800-word pillar post on invoice reconciliation for agencies but only has a rough outline. They paste the brief, pick a GPT-4-class model and a plain-spoken brand voice, and get a structured outline, a full draft, then a polished pass. They spend the afternoon fact-checking figures and adding two real client examples instead of staring at a blank page.

### Weekly tutorials for a solo maintainer

A solo developer maintaining an open-source library wants a weekly tutorial-style post but writes slowly. Using their own OpenAI key on the Pro plan, they generate a draft from a five-bullet brief, keep the code blocks they hand-wrote, and rewrite the model’s explanations where they read as vague. The chain handles headings and transitions; the developer still owns every technical claim before it ships.

### Turning a webinar transcript into a recap

An agency turns a 40-minute client webinar transcript into a long-form recap. They paste the key points into the brief, choose a formal voice to match the client’s brand, and let the outline-draft-polish chain shape it into sections with an intro and FAQ. An editor then checks quotes against the recording and trims anything the model overstated before the client sees it.

### First drafts for a quarter of SEO briefs

An in-house SEO team has twelve approved briefs for the quarter and limited writer hours. They run each brief through the chain to produce first drafts, then route them to human writers for fact-checking, internal links, and original data. The tool removes the cold-start problem; the writers still own accuracy, originality checks, and the final call on what publishes.

## How it works

1. **Connect your LLM key** — Add your own provider key (such as OpenAI) in settings. It is encrypted per user, and all generation usage bills to your provider account, not to ZTools.
2. **Write the brief** — Enter your topic, target keyword, audience, key points, and any sources, then pick the model and a writing voice. The brief and model largely determine output quality.
3. **Generate and edit the outline** — The first prompt in the chain returns a structured heading outline. Review and edit it before drafting, since the draft follows whatever outline you approve.
4. **Draft the article** — The second pass expands your approved outline into a full long-form draft, section by section, in the voice you selected.
5. **Polish the draft** — A final pass tightens phrasing, transitions, and consistency, producing a clean first draft — not a verified, publication-ready article.
6. **Review and fact-check** — Export the draft, verify every claim, statistic, and quote, add sources and internal links, and run an originality check before you publish.

## Tips

- Spend your effort on the brief: list the audience, the exact angle, five to eight key points, and the sources you want referenced. A richer brief does more for quality than switching models.
- Review and edit the outline before drafting — the draft inherits the outline’s gaps, so fixing structure there is far cheaper than rewriting a finished draft.
- Match the model to the job: use a smaller, cheaper model for short drafts to save on your provider bill, and a stronger model for nuanced, long-form pieces.
- Keep a fact-check pass non-negotiable: verify every statistic, quote, and product claim against a primary source, since the tool does not guarantee accuracy.
- Paste real source text — a transcript, report, or notes — into the brief instead of asking the model to recall facts, which reduces invented details.
- Use the Free cap to test your voice and brief format, then move to Pro or Ultimate once you have a repeatable workflow worth scaling.
