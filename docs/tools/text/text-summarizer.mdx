# Text Summarizer

A text summarizer condenses a long document down to its most important sentences using extractive summarization — scoring each sentence by keyword density, position, and relevance to the document's top terms — so readers get the gist in seconds without losing the source's exact wording. The ZTools Text Summarizer runs entirely in your browser using TextRank-style scoring, lets you choose the summary length (5%, 10%, 25%, 50% of original, or a fixed number of sentences), preserves the original sentence wording (no paraphrasing or hallucination), and works offline once loaded.

## Use cases

### Reading-list triage — should I read this article?

Paste a 3,000-word article, pull a 5-sentence summary. Decide in 30 seconds whether to read in full, save for later, or skip.

### Meeting notes and transcripts

A 10,000-word meeting transcript reduced to 20 key sentences. Captures decisions and action items without re-watching the recording.

### Student exam revision

Textbook chapter shrunk to its core 30 sentences. Combined with the original for full review; summary alone for last-minute cram.

### Daily news digest

Paste 5 morning articles, summarize each to 3 sentences. A 60-second briefing instead of 20 minutes of reading.

## How it works

1. **Paste the article or document** — Any size. Plain text or markdown. Multi-paragraph documents work best — short pastes don't leave much to extract.
2. **Choose the summary length** — Percentage of original (5%, 10%, 25%, 50%) or fixed number of sentences (3, 5, 10, 20). Shorter = key points only; longer = more nuance.
3. **Algorithm scores every sentence** — Each sentence gets a score based on: TF-IDF keyword overlap with the document, position (early sentences score higher in news), length (very short or very long sentences are penalized), and relationship to other sentences.
4. **Top sentences are selected and reordered** — Sentences appear in the original document's order — not by score — so the summary reads coherently as a shortened version of the original.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**Is this AI-generated?**</summary>

No — it uses extractive summarization, which selects existing sentences rather than generating new text. No hallucination, no paraphrase errors, but also less natural flow than abstractive AI summarizers.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How is this different from ChatGPT summaries?**</summary>

ChatGPT (and other LLMs) generate new sentences that paraphrase the source. They're smoother but can hallucinate or distort. Extractive summarizers pull verbatim sentences — preserving source accuracy at the cost of natural flow.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What length should I pick?**</summary>

5-10% for skimming/triage. 25% for studying or note-taking. 50% if the original is very dense or you want minimal information loss. A 5-sentence fixed length works well for news.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does it work for non-English text?**</summary>

Yes — the TextRank algorithm is largely language-agnostic since it relies on word co-occurrence rather than language-specific rules. Best results with English; reasonable results with major European languages.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Can it summarize code or structured data?**</summary>

No — algorithms are tuned for prose. For code summaries, use a documentation generator. For data summaries, use the Statistics Calculator.

</details>

## Tips

- For news articles, shorter summaries (5%) are more useful — most news has the thesis in the first paragraph.
- For narrative or story-driven content, summarization often loses the point; read the original.
- Summaries are best for triage and revision, not for replacing primary research.
- Long documents (10k+ words) sometimes need section-by-section summarization rather than whole-document.
