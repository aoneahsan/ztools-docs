# Net Worth Calculator

A net worth calculator is a tool that subtracts everything you owe from everything you own to produce a single figure — your net worth — that captures your overall financial position at one moment. You total your assets (cash, bank balances, investments, retirement accounts, real estate, vehicles, and other property, each at current market value) and your liabilities (mortgage, student and auto loans, credit-card balances, and any other debt, each at its payoff amount), and the difference is the result. It can be negative early in life, which is completely normal. The tool also derives a debt-to-asset ratio, where lower is stronger. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded. This is an estimate, not financial advice.

## Use cases

### Measure real progress instead of income

Income tells you what flows in, but net worth tells you what you actually keep. A high earner who spends everything can have a lower net worth than a modest earner who saves and invests steadily. Recalculating every month or quarter turns an abstract goal into a number you can watch climb, and it rewards paying down debt just as much as growing savings.

### Watch the debt-to-asset ratio, not just the total

Two people can share the same net worth while one is far more exposed, because a large asset pile propped up by heavy borrowing is fragile. The debt-to-asset ratio shows what fraction of what you own is still owed. Tracking it alongside the headline figure highlights whether your position is genuinely strengthening or simply riding rising asset prices.

### See the effect of a big financial decision

Before taking on a mortgage, buying a car, or paying off a loan early, you can enter the before-and-after numbers and see how each choice moves your net worth and debt-to-asset ratio. That makes the trade-off concrete — a new car may add an asset but a larger loan, while an early payoff shrinks both sides — so you can weigh decisions with real figures rather than a hunch.

### Build a repeatable personal balance sheet

Listing assets and liabilities in categories creates a simple personal balance sheet you can reproduce on a schedule. Keeping the same categories each time makes snapshots comparable, so you can spot which parts of your finances are growing and which are stalling. It is the same idea a business uses to track its position, scaled down to a single household.

### Prepare for a loan or planning conversation

Lenders, mortgage brokers, and financial planners often ask for a net-worth summary. Having your assets, liabilities, the total, and the debt-to-asset ratio ready in advance saves time and helps you answer their questions accurately. The figure here is a self-reported estimate rather than a verified financial statement, but it is a solid, organized starting point for that conversation.

## How it works

1. **List your assets at market value** — Enter cash, bank balances, investments, retirement accounts, real estate, vehicles, and other property using what each would sell for today, not what you paid.
2. **List your liabilities at payoff** — Add your mortgage, loans, credit-card balances, and any other debt using the current amount needed to clear each one in full.
3. **Let the tool total each side** — As you type, the calculator sums total assets and total liabilities so you can see both columns at a glance.
4. **Read your net worth** — It subtracts total liabilities from total assets and displays the result, which may be a positive or a negative number.
5. **Check the debt-to-asset ratio** — The tool divides total liabilities by total assets to show how leveraged you are; a lower ratio means a stronger, less debt-dependent position.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**What counts as an asset?**</summary>

Anything you own that has monetary value: cash and bank balances, brokerage and retirement investments, real estate, vehicles, and valuable personal property. Enter each at its current market value — what it would realistically sell for now — not its original purchase price.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What counts as a liability?**</summary>

Any money you owe: mortgage, home-equity, student, auto, and personal loans, outstanding credit-card balances, and unpaid taxes or bills. Use the current payoff balance for each, not the original loan amount.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is a negative net worth bad?**</summary>

Not necessarily. Many people, especially early in a career or just after buying a home, owe more than they own because of student loans or a fresh mortgage. What matters most is whether the number trends upward over time as you pay down debt and build assets.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What is a good debt-to-asset ratio?**</summary>

There is no single right answer; it depends on your age, goals, and the type of debt. As a general idea, a lower ratio means less of what you own is financed by borrowing, which is more resilient. Compare your own ratio over time rather than to a fixed target.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How often should I calculate it?**</summary>

Monthly or quarterly is plenty for most people. Net worth moves slowly, and checking too often can make normal market swings feel dramatic. Using the same categories each time keeps your snapshots comparable.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is my financial data stored or uploaded?**</summary>

No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you enter is sent to a server or saved anywhere by the tool.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is this financial advice?**</summary>

No. It is an estimate to help you organize and track your finances. For decisions about debt, investing, or planning, consult a qualified financial professional who can consider your full situation.

</details>

## Tips

- Use current market values for assets and current payoff balances for debts — mixing old and new figures skews the result.
- Keep the same categories every time so snapshots stay comparable month to month.
- Update home and vehicle values periodically; they drift and can quietly change your net worth.
- Do not double-count — a paycheck you have already spent is not an asset, and a listed loan should appear only once.
- Watch the trend line over months, not a single snapshot — direction matters more than any one figure.
