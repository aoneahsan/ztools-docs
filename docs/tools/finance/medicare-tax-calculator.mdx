# Medicare Tax Calculator

A Medicare tax calculator is a tool that estimates the federal Medicare payroll tax you owe on your earnings for the 2025 tax year. Medicare tax has two parts. The regular tax is 1.45% of all your wages or net self-employment income with no upper wage cap, and an employer matches that 1.45%, so an employee pays 1.45% while the self-employed pay both halves at 2.9%. On top of that, an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies to the individual on earnings above a filing-status threshold. This calculator splits out both parts and shows the totals. It is a 2025 estimate, not tax advice.

## Use cases

### See both halves of the self-employed obligation

For self-employed people, Medicare tax is not withheld by an employer, so both the employee and employer halves fall on you as part of self-employment tax. Entering your net self-employment income shows the full 2.9% regular Medicare portion in one place, which helps when setting aside money for quarterly estimated payments rather than being surprised at filing time.

### Check whether the 0.9% surtax applies to you

The Additional Medicare Tax only kicks in once your earnings pass your filing-status threshold — $200,000 single, $250,000 married filing jointly, or $125,000 married filing separately. Entering your wages and filing status shows whether any of your income crosses that line and how much 0.9% tax the excess generates, so a high earner can anticipate it.

### Reconcile employer withholding on your return

Employers must withhold the extra 0.9% once your wages with them exceed $200,000, no matter your filing status. That can mean too much was withheld against a lower joint gap, or too little across two jobs or a joint household. Seeing the calculated surtax next to what was withheld helps you understand the true-up that happens on Form 8959.

### Separate Medicare tax from the rest of your paycheck

Paystubs lump several taxes together, making it hard to see what Medicare alone costs. This tool isolates just the Medicare piece — regular plus any additional — so you can reason about that line without the noise of income tax or Social Security. For the full paycheck picture, pair it with a dedicated paycheck or payroll calculator.

## How it works

1. **Enter your earnings** — Type your total Medicare wages or net self-employment income for 2025; this is the base the rates apply to.
2. **Choose employee or self-employed** — Employees pay one 1.45% half; the self-employed pay both halves at 2.9%. This sets how the regular tax is computed.
3. **Select your filing status** — Single, head of household, married filing jointly, or married filing separately sets the threshold for the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax.
4. **Add other Medicare wages if any** — If you have wages from another job, or a spouse’s income on a joint return, include them so the threshold is applied to your combined earnings.
5. **Read the breakdown** — The tool shows regular Medicare tax, any 0.9% additional tax on income above the threshold, and the combined total.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**What is the Medicare tax rate for 2025?**</summary>

Regular Medicare tax is 1.45% of all wages or net self-employment income with no wage cap. Employers match it, so an employee’s earnings are effectively taxed 2.9% between the two sides, and the self-employed pay the full 2.9% themselves.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is there a wage cap on Medicare tax?**</summary>

No. Unlike Social Security, Medicare tax has no upper wage limit — the 1.45% applies to every dollar of covered earnings. The only threshold that matters is the one for the extra 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Who pays the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax?**</summary>

Only the individual — the employee or self-employed person. It is never matched by an employer. It applies to earnings above $200,000 (single or head of household), $250,000 (married filing jointly), or $125,000 (married filing separately).

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Why did my employer withhold the extra 0.9%?**</summary>

Employers must start withholding the additional 0.9% once your wages with them pass $200,000 in a year, regardless of your filing status. Because your actual threshold may differ, the amount is reconciled on your tax return via Form 8959.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does this calculate my income tax or Social Security?**</summary>

No. It estimates Medicare tax only. It does not compute federal or state income tax or Social Security tax. Use a paycheck or payroll calculator for the complete picture of your take-home pay.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Do the self-employed really pay double?**</summary>

They pay both the employee and employer halves, totalling 2.9% of net self-employment income, as part of self-employment tax. Half of that self-employment tax is deductible against income tax, which this tool does not model.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is my income data saved anywhere?**</summary>

No. Everything is calculated in your browser. Nothing you enter is uploaded or stored.

</details>

## Tips

- Use your Medicare wages (often higher than taxable-income wages, because pre-tax 401(k) contributions still count) as the base.
- Include a spouse’s earnings when checking the joint threshold — the 0.9% surtax is based on combined income on a joint return.
- If you have two jobs, remember each employer only withholds the 0.9% after $200,000 with them, so you may owe more at filing.
- Set aside the self-employed 2.9% as you earn if you are self-employed, to avoid a large bill at tax time.
- Treat the result as a 2025 estimate and confirm specifics with a tax professional or the IRS Form 8959 instructions.
