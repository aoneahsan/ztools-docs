# Business Plan Template

A business plan template is a tool that assembles a structured, section-by-section business plan from the details you enter, arranging them into the nine parts lenders and investors expect: executive summary, company description, products and services, market analysis, marketing and sales, organization and management, funding request, financial projections, and an appendix. Where a section is blank, it inserts a plain-language guidance prompt so you know what belongs there, producing a ready-to-edit outline rather than a finished document. It runs entirely in your browser, saves nothing to a server, and is a planning template — not financial or legal advice. Validate every assumption and figure before you share it with anyone.

## Use cases

### Draft a plan for a bank loan or SBA application

Lenders want to see a specific set of sections in a predictable order before they will consider a loan. Filling the template gives you every heading a bank or SBA reviewer expects — company description, market analysis, management, funding request, and projections — so nothing obvious is missing. You still supply the real figures and a credible repayment story, but the structure reviewers scan for is already in place.

### Pitch to investors with a clear narrative

Investors read many decks and plans, so a logical flow matters. The template orders your executive summary first, then builds the case section by section toward the funding request and projections, helping your story read as a coherent argument rather than scattered notes. Use the guidance prompts to make sure each section actually answers the question an investor is asking about traction, market, and returns.

### Organize a rough idea into something reviewable

Many founders have the idea in their head but freeze at the blank page. Entering what you know — even partially — turns loose thoughts into an outline with clearly labelled gaps, so you can see exactly what still needs research. Blank sections carry a prompt describing what belongs there, which makes the plan feel like a checklist to work through instead of an intimidating essay.

### Create a working document you keep updating

A business plan is not a one-time deliverable; it changes as you learn. Because the output is a plain, ready-to-edit outline, you can revise numbers, swap in real market data, and expand sections over time in your own editor. Treat the first pass as a skeleton and return to strengthen the weakest sections as your business takes shape and the assumptions get tested.

### Prepare for a class, incubator, or grant

Business courses, accelerators, and grant programs often require a written plan in a standard shape. The template covers the conventional nine sections, giving you a compliant starting structure you can tailor to a specific program’s requirements. Always check the program’s own template or rubric, since some ask for extra sections, a particular order, or a strict page limit you will need to respect.

## How it works

1. **Enter your company basics** — Fill in the business name, what it does, and its stage so the header and company-description sections have a foundation.
2. **Describe products, market, and strategy** — Add your products or services, target market and competitors, and how you will reach customers, one section at a time.
3. **Add the team and funding request** — List key people and their roles, then state how much funding you seek and how it will be used, if you are raising.
4. **Drop in the numbers you have** — Enter any revenue, cost, or projection figures you know; leave the rest for later — blank fields keep their guidance prompt.
5. **Generate and edit the outline** — The tool assembles every section in order, inserts prompts where you left gaps, and gives you a ready-to-edit plan to refine.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**Is this a substitute for a real business plan?**</summary>

It produces the structure and prompts of one, but the substance — real market data, honest figures, and your strategy — is yours to supply. Treat the output as a strong starting outline you then research, fill in, and refine, not a finished plan you can submit as-is.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What sections does it include?**</summary>

The nine conventional parts: executive summary, company description, products and services, market analysis, marketing and sales, organization and management, funding request, financial projections, and an appendix. You can delete or add sections in your editor afterward.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Can I use it if I do not have financials yet?**</summary>

Yes. Leave the financial fields blank and those sections keep a guidance prompt describing what to add later. Many early founders start this way and fill in projections once they have researched their costs and pricing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is this financial or legal advice?**</summary>

No. It is a general planning template, not financial, investment, or legal advice. Assumptions and projections should be validated, and anything with legal or tax implications should be reviewed by a qualified professional before you rely on it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Will lenders or investors accept this format?**</summary>

The section structure follows what most lenders and investors expect, but each has its own preferences and some programs require a specific template. Check the recipient’s requirements and adapt the outline to match before you send it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is my information uploaded anywhere?**</summary>

No. The plan is assembled in your browser and nothing you type is sent to a server or stored elsewhere. Save the generated text yourself to keep a copy.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How long should the finished plan be?**</summary>

It depends on the audience — a lender may want a detailed document while an early investor prefers a tight summary. Focus on clarity over length, and let each section be as long as it needs to make its point well.

</details>

## Tips

- Write the executive summary last, even though it appears first — it is easier once the other sections exist.
- Replace every guidance prompt before sharing the plan; leftover prompt text signals an unfinished draft.
- Back up your numbers with sources in the market-analysis section rather than stating figures unsupported.
- Keep projections conservative and label them as estimates — reviewers distrust numbers that look too optimistic.
- Save versions as your business evolves so you can show how the plan matured over time.
