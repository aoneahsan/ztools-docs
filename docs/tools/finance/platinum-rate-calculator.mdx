# platinum-rate-calculator

A platinum rate calculator computes the metal value of platinum jewelry, coins, or bars by combining weight, purity (most jewelry-grade platinum is .950 — 95% pure — though .900 and .850 are used in some markets), and the current platinum spot price. The ZTools Platinum Rate Calculator runs entirely in the browser, supports gram / ounce / kilogram inputs, all common purity stamps, and includes notes on why platinum and gold prices have decoupled in recent years (industrial demand for catalytic converters, supply concentration in South Africa, less consumer-jewelry demand than gold) — context that affects whether melt value is the right benchmark.

## Use cases

### Selling platinum jewelry

Wedding rings and engagement settings often use .950 platinum. Calculate melt to evaluate buyer offers.

### Estimating catalytic-converter scrap

Recyclers pay for the platinum / palladium / rhodium content. Weight × purity × spot gives a rough metal value for negotiation.

### Investment-grade bullion bars

Platinum bars (.9995 / .9999) trade at small premiums to spot. Weight × spot gives the floor price.

### Estate / inheritance valuation

Distinguishing platinum from white gold matters — they look similar but values differ. Stamp + density check + this calculator clarifies.

## How it works

1. **Enter spot platinum price** — Per gram or per troy ounce. Platinum has often traded below gold since ~2015 — verify the current rate.
2. **Pick weight unit** — Grams, ounces, kilograms.
3. **Enter purity** — .950 (standard jewelry), .900 (some hallmarks), .850 (lower jewelry standard), .9995 (investment bars).
4. **Compute melt value** — weight × purity × spot price.
5. **Compare to gold equivalent** — Optional: see what equivalent gold would cost for the same weight; useful when pricing custom rings.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**Why does platinum sometimes trade below gold?**</summary>

Platinum demand is heavily industrial (catalytic converters); when auto demand softens or palladium substitutes, platinum drops. Gold has consistent investment / jewelry demand.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How can I tell platinum from white gold?**</summary>

Stamp ("PT", "Plat", "950") plus density. Platinum is significantly denser (21.45 g/cc) than white gold (~14–16 g/cc). XRF or acid testing confirms.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is platinum jewelry hypoallergenic?**</summary>

Generally yes — platinum alloys typically use ruthenium, iridium, or cobalt rather than nickel, avoiding the most common metal allergies.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Why is the buyer offering only 50% of spot?**</summary>

Platinum buyers take large margins because the market is illiquid for jewelry. Specialty refiners pay closer to 90% of spot but require minimum lots.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Can I refine platinum at home?**</summary>

No — process involves aqua regia and is dangerous. Send to a professional refiner.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does the calculator handle platinum-iridium alloys?**</summary>

Yes — enter the platinum percentage as the purity. Iridium has its own value but is small; calculator rounds in your favour by ignoring it.

</details>

## Tips

- Confirm the stamp before trusting purity — "PLAT" alone in the US implies at least .950; international standards vary.
- Platinum is heavy — check density as a quick authenticity test before shipping for sale.
- Spot prices for platinum are noisier than gold; lock at the moment of transaction.
- For catalytic-converter scrap, the small piece often contains more total precious metal than visible — weigh the whole substrate.
- For investment bars, prefer recognised refiners (PAMP, Valcambi) — third-party bars trade at deeper discounts.
