# silver-rate-calculator

A silver rate calculator computes the metal value of a silver item by combining weight, purity (fineness — millesimal parts of pure silver per 1000 by weight), and the current silver spot price, giving the raw melt value before refining or dealer margins. The ZTools Silver Rate Calculator runs entirely in the browser, supports common purity standards (.999 fine, sterling .925, coin silver .900, Britannia .958), gram / ounce / kilogram inputs, and is useful for buying or selling jewelry, estimating sterling flatware, valuing coin collections, or insuring a household silver inventory.

## Use cases

### Selling sterling flatware

Inherited sterling tea service. Weigh it; enter sterling .925; multiply by spot. Now you know what dealers should be offering.

### Coin-silver collections

Pre-1965 US dimes / quarters are 90% silver. Total weight times 0.9 times spot gives melt value — often well above face value.

### Pure-silver bars

Investment bars are .999 fine; weight × spot gives intrinsic value before any premium for collectible bars.

### Insurance estimation for household silver

For non-collectible items, melt-value-plus-markup is a defensible insurance figure; for antiques, an appraiser is still needed.

## How it works

1. **Enter spot silver price** — Per gram or per troy ounce in local currency. Silver is more volatile than gold — refresh before quoting.
2. **Pick weight unit** — Grams, troy ounces (31.1035 g), or kilograms (preferred for bars).
3. **Enter purity** — .999 fine (investment grade), .925 sterling (jewelry / flatware), .900 coin (US pre-1965), .958 Britannia, custom percentage for unusual stamps.
4. **Compute melt value** — weight × purity × spot price.
5. **Add premium (optional)** — Bars and rounds carry a premium (5–15%) over melt — the calculator can layer it on for buying decisions.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**Why does silver pricing vary so much?**</summary>

Silver is more volatile than gold — daily moves of 2–5% are common. Industrial demand (electronics, solar) drives much of it; precious-metal sentiment drives the rest.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What is the difference between fine silver and sterling?**</summary>

Fine silver is .999 pure (very soft, mostly used for investment bars). Sterling silver is .925 — 92.5% silver, 7.5% copper for hardness. Most jewelry and flatware is sterling.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Are silver-plated items worth anything?**</summary>

Almost nothing in melt value — plating is microns thin. Worth depends on the underlying base metal and any antique / craftsmanship value.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How do I know if my piece is real silver?**</summary>

Look for hallmarks: "925", "Sterling", ".999". Magnetic test (silver is non-magnetic) is a quick screen. Acid test or XRF gun for confirmation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How are coin-silver values calculated?**</summary>

Pre-1965 US dimes/quarters/halves are .900 silver, weight ratio per coin known. Total face value × 0.715 × silver spot/oz approximates melt for typical worn coins.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Should I sell or hold silver?**</summary>

Depends on your view of the market — not financial advice from this calculator. Dollar-cost-average buy and long hold has historically worked for stackers; trading silver actively requires more skill.

</details>

## Tips

- Get an accurate jewelry scale; kitchen scales accumulate error on small pieces.
- For coin silver, weigh the lot rather than counting — tarnish + wear changes per-coin weight.
- Sterling makers put marks beyond ".925" — research the maker for potential antique premium.
- Spot prices are more volatile than gold; lock pricing the day you transact, not weeks earlier.
- Dealer offers run 60–85% of melt for jewelry / flatware; closer to 95–99% for investment-grade bars.
