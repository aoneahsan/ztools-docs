# W-4 Withholding Calculator

A W-4 withholding calculator is a tool that estimates your 2025 federal income tax from the entries on Form W-4 and works out how much should be withheld from each paycheck so you neither owe a large bill nor over-withhold. It adds your salary and other income (line 4a), subtracts the standard deduction and extra deductions (line 4b), applies the 2025 brackets, and takes off $2,000 per qualifying child. Dividing that yearly tax by your pay periods gives a per-paycheck target, and if your withholding falls short it suggests a line 4(c) extra amount. It models federal income tax and the Child Tax Credit only — not FICA, state tax, or the multiple-jobs worksheet — a TY2025 estimate, not tax advice.

## Use cases

### Fill out a new W-4 with confidence

Starting a job or updating your W-4 after a life change means guessing at boxes that affect every paycheck. This calculator turns those entries into a concrete number — the federal tax you are likely to owe and what should come out per pay period — so you can complete the form knowing roughly where you will land at tax time instead of guessing blindly.

### Find the right line 4(c) extra amount

If you tend to owe at filing, Form W-4 line 4(c) lets you add a flat extra amount to each paycheck. The tool compares your projected tax to what is currently being withheld and, when there is a shortfall, suggests a per-paycheck extra that would close the gap by year-end, so you can enter a specific figure rather than a round guess.

### Avoid over-withholding and a big refund

A large refund feels nice but means too much was withheld all year. By showing the per-paycheck amount that would break even, the calculator helps you keep more money in each check if you would rather not lend it to the IRS interest-free. You can then trim or remove a line 4(c) amount and see the effect on the target.

### Account for a second income or spouse

Line 4a captures other income not from jobs, and this tool folds it into the estimate so withholding covers it. It is honest about a limit, though: it does not run the W-4 multiple-jobs worksheet for two-earner households, so couples with two similar salaries should treat the single-job estimate as a starting point and verify with the IRS worksheet.

### Pair it with the Paycheck Calculator

This tool focuses on federal income tax and the line 4(c) target, while the Paycheck Calculator breaks down FICA, take-home, and deductions. Using the W-4 estimate to set your withholding and then the Paycheck Calculator to see the resulting net pay gives a fuller picture than either alone, since neither one models state tax on its own.

## How it works

1. **Choose your filing status** — Pick single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, or head of household to set the 2025 standard deduction and brackets.
2. **Enter salary and pay frequency** — Type your annual salary and how many paychecks you get per year — for example 26 for biweekly, 24 for semimonthly.
3. **Add W-4 lines 4a and 4b** — Enter other income (4a) that raises taxable income and extra deductions (4b) that lower it, if you have them.
4. **Enter qualifying children** — Add the number of children under 17 so the tool applies the $2,000-per-child Child Tax Credit.
5. **Read the target and any 4(c) amount** — The tool shows estimated annual tax, the per-paycheck target, and a suggested line 4(c) extra when your current withholding is short.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**Which tax year does it use?**</summary>

It uses 2025 federal tax figures — the 2025 standard deductions, brackets, and the $2,000 Child Tax Credit — to estimate income tax for a US filer. It does not compute state or local withholding.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does it include Social Security, Medicare, or take-home pay?**</summary>

No. This tool estimates federal income tax withholding only. FICA (Social Security and Medicare), state tax, and full net-pay math are not modeled here — use the Paycheck Calculator for take-home and payroll deductions.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What is Form W-4 line 4(c)?**</summary>

Line 4(c) is the box on Form W-4 where you request an additional flat dollar amount withheld from each paycheck. If your other entries would leave you under-withheld, adding a 4(c) amount raises withholding to help you break even. The calculator suggests one when there is a shortfall.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Can it handle two jobs or a working spouse?**</summary>

Only partially. You can add other income on line 4a, but the tool does not run the W-4 multiple-jobs worksheet, which coordinates withholding across two or more jobs. Two-earner households should use the estimate as a starting point and check the IRS worksheet or estimator.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How is the per-paycheck target calculated?**</summary>

The tool estimates your full-year federal income tax after the standard deduction, extra deductions, and Child Tax Credit, then divides that by the number of pay periods you enter. That even split is a simplification — real payroll withholding uses IRS percentage-method tables per paycheck.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**How does this differ from the Paycheck Calculator?**</summary>

They complement each other. This W-4 tool focuses on income-tax withholding and the line 4(c) amount to enter on your form; the Paycheck Calculator shows the resulting take-home pay including FICA and other deductions. Use this one to set withholding, that one to see the net effect.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is my salary information uploaded?**</summary>

No. All calculations happen in your browser. Nothing you type is sent to a server or saved after you close the page.

</details>

## Tips

- Enter your number of pay periods correctly — 26 for biweekly, 24 for semimonthly, 12 for monthly.
- Use line 4a for other income such as interest or a side gig so withholding covers it.
- Add a line 4(c) amount if the tool shows a shortfall and you want to avoid owing at filing.
- For two jobs or a working spouse, verify with the IRS multiple-jobs worksheet — this tool does not run it.
- Re-check after a raise or bonus, since a higher salary can move you into a new bracket.
