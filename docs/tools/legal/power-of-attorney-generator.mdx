# Power of Attorney Generator

A power of attorney generator is a tool that assembles a general or limited (special) power of attorney — a document in which a principal authorizes an agent, also called an attorney-in-fact, to act on their behalf. A general power grants broad authority; a limited one restricts the agent to specific tasks you define. An optional durability clause lets the authority survive the principal’s incapacity, which is often the whole point of the document. Everything is generated in your browser and nothing is uploaded. This is a general template, not legal advice: execution requirements — notarization, witnesses, statutory forms, and durability wording — vary by state and carry real consequences, so attorney review is strongly recommended.

## Use cases

### Authorize someone to handle a single transaction

Sometimes you just need one person to sign for you on one thing — closing a property sale while you are abroad, or collecting a document on your behalf. A limited power of attorney names the agent and spells out exactly that task and nothing more. The generator produces a focused document you can define narrowly, which many people prefer over granting broad authority for a one-off need.

### Plan for incapacity with a durable power

A durable power of attorney keeps your agent’s authority in effect if you later become unable to make decisions yourself, which is why it is central to many incapacity plans. Adding the durability clause produces language intended to survive incapacity. Because the exact wording that makes a POA durable is set by state law, this is precisely the kind of document to have an attorney confirm.

### Let a trusted person manage affairs while you are away

If you will be traveling, deployed, or otherwise unreachable, a general power of attorney can let a trusted family member or friend manage financial and administrative matters in your absence. The template names your agent and grants the broad authority a general POA carries. Choose your agent carefully — a general power is powerful and should go only to someone you fully trust.

### Prepare a draft to discuss with an attorney

Rather than arriving at a lawyer’s office empty-handed, you can generate a draft that already names the principal and agent and states the scope and durability you want. That gives the attorney a clear starting point to adapt to your state’s execution rules — notary and witness requirements, and any statutory form — making the review quicker and more focused.

### Understand the roles before you commit

The document makes the relationship concrete: the principal grants authority, and the agent (attorney-in-fact) must act within it. Generating a sample helps you see what powers you would be handing over and to whom, so you can think carefully before signing. A power of attorney is consequential and easy to misuse, so clarity about scope matters before anything is executed.

## How it works

1. **Name the principal** — Enter the full legal name and address of the person granting authority — that is you, the principal.
2. **Name the agent (attorney-in-fact)** — Enter the full name and address of the person you are authorizing to act on your behalf; choose someone you trust completely.
3. **Choose general or limited** — Select a general power for broad authority, or a limited (special) power and describe the specific tasks the agent may perform.
4. **Decide on durability** — Turn the durability clause on if the authority should survive your incapacity, or leave it off for a non-durable power.
5. **Generate, then execute properly** — The tool assembles the document, but you must sign it under your state’s rules — often before a notary and witnesses — after attorney review.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>**What is an attorney-in-fact?**</summary>

It is simply the person you authorize to act for you — your agent. The term does not mean the person is a lawyer. The principal is the one granting authority; the attorney-in-fact is the one receiving it and must act within its scope.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What does "durable" mean?**</summary>

A durable power of attorney stays in effect if you become incapacitated, whereas a non-durable one generally ends at that point. The specific language that makes a POA durable is set by state law, so the wording matters and should be confirmed by an attorney.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**What is the difference between general and limited?**</summary>

A general power of attorney grants broad authority to act across many matters; a limited (or special) power restricts the agent to specific tasks you define. Limited powers are common when you need help with just one transaction.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Does a power of attorney need to be notarized?**</summary>

Often, yes — many states require notarization, witnesses, or a particular statutory form for a POA to be valid, especially for real estate or durable powers. Requirements vary by state, so check yours and have the document reviewed before signing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is this legal advice?**</summary>

No. This is a general template, not legal advice. A power of attorney is consequential and easy to misuse or invalidate, and execution rules differ by state, so an attorney should review the document for your situation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Can I cancel a power of attorney later?**</summary>

Generally a principal with capacity can revoke a power of attorney, usually in writing and by notifying the agent and any relevant institutions. The exact revocation process depends on state law, so confirm the correct steps for where you live.

</details>

<details>
<summary>**Is my information kept private?**</summary>

Yes. The document is assembled in your browser and the names, addresses, and other details you enter are not uploaded or stored on any server.

</details>

## Tips

- Choose an agent you trust without reservation — a power of attorney can be misused by the wrong person.
- Prefer a limited power scoped to the exact task when you only need help with one thing.
- Confirm your state’s notary and witness requirements before signing, or the document may be rejected.
- Name a successor agent in case your first choice is unable or unwilling to serve.
- Give copies only to the institutions that need them, and keep a record of who holds one.
